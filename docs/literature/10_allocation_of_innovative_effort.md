# Strand 10 — The allocation of innovative effort, and innovation that does not raise productivity

Version 0.1, 2026-11-17. Register IDs: L177 (Baumol 1990), L005/L144 (Baumol 1967), L007, L073, L094, L178, L179–L187. Open questions: OQ-87, OQ-88, OQ-89. Related: theory note 04 §5.3 and §6; planned DENUE study (D-071).

This note records a reading of Baumol (1990) and a discussion of it with the project's author on 2026-11-17. The author's own views are attributed to him (PW) and kept apart from the literature; the classification of innovative activity they lead to is not settled (OQ-87).

## Baumol (1990)

Baumol's "Entrepreneurship: productive, unproductive, and destructive" (*Journal of Political Economy* 98(5): 893–921; L177) should not be confused with his work on unbalanced growth and the cost disease (Baumol and Bowen 1966; Baumol 1967, L005/L144), which is the subject of theory note 04. The 1990 paper argues that the supply of entrepreneurs, people ingenious and creative in finding ways to add to their own wealth, power and prestige, varies little across societies, while the rules of the game that determine the relative payoffs to different activities vary a great deal. Those rules decide whether entrepreneurial effort goes into productive activity (innovation, imitation of better methods), unproductive activity (rent-seeking, litigation, the pursuit of office) or destructive activity (warfare, predation). Policy should therefore act on the rules rather than on the supply of entrepreneurship. The evidence is historical: in ancient Rome wealth and status came from land, lending and political office, and commerce was left largely to freedmen; in imperial China the examinations and office in the bureaucracy were the route to wealth, and private property was insecure; in early medieval Europe military conquest was a principal form of enterprise. ## Innovation outside the economy, and why Roman inventions did not spread (PW)

PW prefers "innovation" to Baumol's "entrepreneurship" and reads the historical cases as follows. Over most of history, innovative effort faced incentive structures in which business was a low-status activity and the prizes were religious, political, military or landed; much innovation therefore produced results that were not economic. This may help explain why Roman inventions did not transfer into production: Hero of Alexandria's steam device remained a curiosity, and the story of an unbreakable glass whose inventor was put to death (Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 36.195; Petronius, *Satyricon* 51) describes an invention that was suppressed and forgotten. Innovation takes many forms that are not channelled through economic incentives (in art, music, sport, food, religion, war), with spillovers between them and production. PW's conclusion for this project is that the rules of the game should encourage productive innovation, if necessary at the expense of unproductive and destructive forms.

Two qualifications from the literature refine this reading. First, payoffs are not the only condition for an invention to be adopted. Allen (2009; L179) argues that the steam engine was developed in Britain because labour was expensive relative to coal there; in Rome slave labour was abundant and cheap mineral fuel was not. Adoption also needed complementary capabilities (in the British case, the precision metalworking to bore cylinders), and Mokyr (2016; L180) stresses institutions that codify and circulate useful knowledge, which Rome lacked. Productive innovation spreads when four conditions hold together: payoffs, relative prices that make adoption worthwhile, complementary capabilities, and the diffusion of knowledge. Second, the glass story is probably apocryphal; as told, it is an example of an incumbent protecting a rent by suppressing an innovation, which is Baumol's destructive category rather than an instance of non-economic innovation.

Spillovers from non-economic innovation are documented (monasteries and the spread of water mills and mechanical clocks; military demand and metallurgy or navigation), but in each case they reached production through an actor who adopted the knowledge commercially or through the state, so they are subject to the same four conditions.

## Innovation that does not raise productivity

PW's intuition is that many types of innovation are neutral for productivity and growth. The literature supports this, and suggests that the distinction cuts across Baumol's, which classifies activities (production against the pursuit of office or litigation). An innovation can instead be classified by its effect on the economy's possibilities: it can save resources (the same final goods with fewer inputs); add variety or quality that consumers value (a welfare gain that appears in measured output only if statistics adjust for quality); redistribute (take market share or rents from others without lowering costs); or destroy. Innovation inside business and in response to economic incentives can fall in the third group. Examples are advertising that shifts customers between close substitutes, the race among trading firms to be faster by microseconds, which Budish, Cramton and Shim (2015; L181) show mainly transfers profits, and innovation in positional goods (Hirsch 1976; L182). When entry mainly takes customers from incumbents, free entry produces too many firms (Mankiw and Whinston 1986; L183), and in Schumpeterian growth models part of the private return to innovation is such a transfer.

## Intermediation: does adding a link to the supply chain raise productivity?

PW asks whether it is better for consumers to buy from producers directly or through retail, and what the growth benefits are of adding a link to a supply chain. The answer turns on whether the link lowers the total real cost of delivering goods from producers to consumers, including consumers' own costs of search, travel, storage and risk. A link can lower that cost by specialisation and scale (a retailer serving many producers sells, stores and matches more cheaply than each producer could, an instance of the division of labour deepening with the extent of the market, Young 1928, L184), by economising on search and matching (Rubinstein and Wolinsky 1987; L185), or, in expanding-variety models, by adding intermediate inputs that raise productivity (Ethier 1982, L186; Romer 1990, L007). The evidence shows that retail innovation can be productive: in United States retail in the 1990s nearly all productivity growth came from the entry of more productive establishments of national chains and the exit of less productive single stores (Foster, Haltiwanger and Krizan 2006; L187), and in Mexico the entry of foreign supermarkets raised household welfare across the income distribution, mainly through lower prices, including at surviving traditional stores (Atkin, Faber and Gonzalez-Navarro 2018; L094). A link created by a barrier (an exclusive distributor, a licensed intermediary that cannot be bypassed, a permit holder) raises the final price by its margin and reduces quantities, and any labour it employs is wasted.

National accounts do not separate the two cases: both appear as value added in distributive trade, measured by the margin. This is the same problem as in theory note 04 §5.3, where employment growth in high-margin sectors raises the static reallocation term without any fall in real costs. Whether a given link raises productivity is an empirical question for each market, best answered by the change in delivered prices when an intermediary enters or is removed (OQ-88).

## Demand multipliers and growth

PW asks whether, in a closed economy, additional expenditure on such services is multiplicative. Two mechanisms should be distinguished. With unemployed resources, any additional expenditure raises income by a multiple that depends on the propensity to spend of those who receive it, not on whether the service is useful; at full employment the multiplier disappears and the resources the link uses are withdrawn from other uses. Long-run growth depends on factor accumulation and productivity, so the composition of spending affects growth in a closed economy only if sectors differ in dynamic returns (learning by doing, scale economies, the Kaldor–Verdoorn relation, L003), which are usually found to be weaker in traditional services than in manufacturing. The local multiplier of tradable on non-tradable employment (Moretti 2010; L073) does not operate in a closed economy, where nothing is sold outside. One exception is important in Latin America: when the additional link employs workers whose alternative is open or disguised unemployment, in Lewis's sense, it adds output at a social cost close to zero, although its productivity is low. Informal retail plays this role, and in the decomposition it lowers the static term (note 04 §5.3) while raising output relative to idleness (OQ-89).

## Implications for the project

The planned DENUE classification (D-071) separates innovating, rent-dependent and replicative establishments and implicitly treats innovation as resource-saving. The discussion above suggests a fourth category, innovation that redistributes (new formats, marketing or financial products that take share without lowering costs), which location and sector data cannot separate from resource-saving innovation. The price response to the entry of a new format can (OQ-88). How to divide innovative activity, and how to evaluate each type, is left open (OQ-87).
