# Strand 9 — The middle-income trap, and how this project relates to it

Register IDs: L043, L065, L070, L104–L112.

## 1. The literature in four positions

**(a) The trap exists, and it is about switching growth models.** The term comes from Gill & Kharas (2007, L104). The idea is that strategies that take a country from low to middle income stop working. These strategies are cheap labor, capital accumulation, imitation and moving workers out of agriculture. Growth past middle income needs innovation, skills and better institutions. Acemoglu, Aghion & Zilibotti (L065) give the formal version: investment-based growth suits countries far from the frontier, and selection and innovation matter near it. The World Bank's *WDR 2024* (L043) is the current flagship statement: 1i → 2i → 3i.

**(b) Growth slowdowns cluster at middle income.** Fast growers tend to slow around US$10–11k and US$15–16k (2005 PPP). A high share of high-tech exports and more secondary and tertiary education reduce the risk (Eichengreen, Park & Shin 2012, 2013; L070, L105).

**(c) There is no trap in the data.** Transition-matrix analysis gives little support for a middle-income trap (Im & Rosenblatt 2013, L106). Bulman, Eden & Nguyen (2017, L107) find no unusual stagnation at any middle-income level, though growth drivers differ between low and high income. Pritchett & Summers (2014, L108) argue that regression to the mean is a more robust feature of growth than any trap.

**(d) The trap is political and institutional.** Doner & Schneider (2016, L109) argue that economists agree on what to do (skills, R&D, infrastructure, institutions). The upgrading institutions are costly, long-term and coordination-heavy, and the coalitions needed to build them are weak in middle-income countries. The causes are fragmented business (multinationals that do R&D at home, diversified business groups in commodities and oligopolies), labor fragmented by informality, and inequality that fuels clientelism. Schneider (2013, L110) calls the Latin American variant *hierarchical capitalism*. Its four features are multinationals, diversified business groups, low skills and segmented labor markets, held together by institutional complementarities.

Two theoretical models with explicit multiple equilibria are close to this project:
- **Agénor & Canuto (2015, L111):** occupational choice, a knowledge externality from diversification, and a low-growth steady state with talent misallocation. Access to advanced infrastructure is the escape route.
- **Lee (2013, L112):** catch-up countries escaped by specializing in short-cycle technologies. Their knowledge becomes obsolete quickly, so incumbents' advantage matters less.

## 2. Where this project agrees, and what it adds

**Latin America is the canonical case.** Most countries described as "trapped" are Latin American. They reached middle income by mid-century and have not converged since. The puzzle the project calls Q4 is the middle-income trap question in its sharpest form.

**Position (c) helps the project, not hurts it.** If there is no generic trap at a given income level, then Latin America's stagnation is *not* a universal feature of middle income. It needs a region-specific explanation. Kremer, Willis & You (L044) make this sharper: unconditional convergence has been the global norm since about 2000, and Latin America is a laggard in it.

**Relative to (a), the project is more specific about the "switch".** The standard story is a switch from imitation to innovation. The project's evidence suggests Latin America's problem is earlier and more mundane. It is a failure of *intensive-margin* technology adoption inside existing firms (L041, L045). That is a 2i problem in WDR terms, not a 3i one. Recommending more R&D (3i) to economies whose firms do not absorb existing technology is premature (cf. Cirera & Maloney, L046).

**Relative to (d), the project provides the economic micro-mechanisms behind the political story.** Doner–Schneider and Schneider describe complementarities between firm structure, skills and segmented labor markets. The project's Idea 4 loops are the economic version of the same complementarities:

| Doner & Schneider / Schneider feature | Project mechanism | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Labor segmented by informality; insiders vs. outsiders | Fiscal loop: formality wedge ↔ narrow tax base; S/P/E informality | Idea 4 loop 2; WP02 |
| Low skills; weak demand for skills from firms | Capability loop: few M-segment jobs → little on-the-job learning → few managers/entrepreneurs | Idea 3; L102, L103 |
| Diversified business groups in commodities and oligopolies | Incumbent–competition loop; commodity–capability ratchet | Idea 4 loops 3–4 |
| Multinationals doing R&D at home, little local upgrading | Foreign firms' profit composition, supplier linkages, FX claim of domestic-market FDI | WP03 |

In short, the political-economy literature explains *why reform coalitions don't form*. The project tries to explain *why the economic equilibrium they would need to change is self-reinforcing*, and which links are weakest. The two are complements.

**Relative to (b), the project offers an interpretation.** High-tech export share and tertiary education predict avoiding slowdowns. In the project's terms, these are proxies for tradability and capability building. They are two of the five properties in Idea 2, the properties that let an economy keep productivity growth and external balance together.

## 3. What is distinctive, and what is not

**Not distinctive.** Several ideas are well established:
- multiple equilibria at middle income (Agénor & Canuto);
- complementarities sustaining Latin America's low equilibrium (Schneider);
- the political obstacles (Doner & Schneider).

**Potentially distinctive.** Three contributions may be new:
1. **The "segment trap".** Latin America is trapped less by its *sector* mix than by its *segment* mix: a persistently large T-segment, sustained by the fiscal and capability loops. The evidence from Mexico (Fentanes & Levy, L101) is consistent with this. A multi-country test would link the middle-income trap literature to the informality and misallocation literatures, which currently barely cite each other.
2. **The external constraint as a trap mechanism.** The middle-income trap literature is overwhelmingly supply-side. WP01 and WP03 add a demand and balance-of-payments channel. Growth episodes end because domestic-market growth, including domestic-market FDI, raises foreign-exchange claims faster than tradable capacity grows. This fits the region's history of growth episodes ending in external crises.
3. **The commodity–capability ratchet.** Repeated commodity cycles may ratchet tradable capability downward. That would explain why resource-rich Latin American countries stagnated while resource-rich advanced economies (Australia, Norway, Canada) did not.

## 4. Implications for the planned model (WP04)

The minimal model should be positioned as a middle-income trap model. Agénor & Canuto (2015) is the closest formal benchmark. The model should show three things:
1. a low equilibrium driven by segment composition rather than sector composition;
2. that removing one loop (e.g., the formality wedge) is insufficient without tradable growth;
3. that commodity shocks can move an economy between basins of attraction.

These are the three points on which it would differ from the existing trap models.
