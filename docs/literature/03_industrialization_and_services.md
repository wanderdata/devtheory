# Strand 3 — Industrialization, premature deindustrialization, and services

Register IDs: L003, L005, L020–L023, L056, L066, L067.

## Why manufacturing was special

- **Kaldor (L003):** increasing returns in manufacturing make it the engine of aggregate productivity growth.
- **Unconditional convergence (Rodrik L020):** formal manufacturing industries converge to the frontier regardless of country fundamentals — an "escalator" that works even with weak institutions.
- **Employment matters (Felipe, Mehta & Rhee L067):** countries that became rich typically passed through high manufacturing *employment* shares; output shares predict less.
- **Tradability:** manufacturing output faces world demand, so productivity growth does not hit a domestic demand ceiling (unlike non-tradable services, where Baumol L005 applies).

## Premature deindustrialization

- **Fact (Rodrik L021):** the manufacturing hump has shifted down and left — later developers peak at lower shares and lower incomes. Latin America has been especially hard hit; Asian manufactures exporters were insulated. Drivers: globalization (competition from Asia) and labor-saving technology.
- **Theory 1 — technology gaps (Fujiwara & Matsuyama L022):** with the Baumol mechanism, late adopters of sectoral technologies industrialize later and peak lower; cross-country productivity dispersion is largest in agriculture, smallest in manufacturing.
- **Theory 2 — heterogeneous catch-up (Huneeus & Rogerson L023):** differences in agricultural productivity growth explain most variation in peak industrial shares.
- **Implication for LAC:** early peaks need not mean a policy failure in manufacturing per se; they can result from slow agricultural and services catch-up combined with globalization. But the *consequence* — losing the escalator before reaching high income — is real.

## Can services substitute?

- **Skill-intensive services drive the modern service economy (Buera & Kaboski L066).**
- **Services heterogeneity (Nayyar, Hallward-Driemeier & Davies L056):** ICT, professional, financial and logistics services share manufacturing's features (scale, innovation, spillovers, tradability) but are skill-intensive; low-skill services absorb labor with little productivity growth.
- **Upgrading labor-absorbing services (Rodrik & Sandhu L049):** since manufacturing can't absorb the urban labor force, raising productivity in petty services is unavoidable; strategies include expanding large productive firms, public inputs to small firms, labor-complementing technologies, and training with wrap-around support.

## Synthesis for the project

Manufacturing's developmental value came from a **bundle** of properties that happened to coincide: (i) a large productivity premium over the traditional sector, (ii) fast, unconditional productivity growth, (iii) high absorption of low-skill labor, (iv) tradability (no demand ceiling), (v) on-the-job learning and organizational capability building. Today the bundle has come apart: modern manufacturing keeps (ii) and (iv) but loses (iii); tradable services have (i), (ii), (iv) but not (iii); labor-absorbing services have (iii) but not (ii) or (iv). This is the basis of Idea 2 ("unbundling of the escalator") in the framework.
