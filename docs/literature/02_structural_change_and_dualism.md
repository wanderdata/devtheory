# Strand 2 — Structural change and the dual economy

Register IDs: L002, L006, L018, L019, L024–L027.

## Core ideas

**Lewis dual economy (L002).** Development is the transfer of labor from a traditional, low-productivity sector to a modern, capitalist one. Growth comes from the productivity gap times the flow of labor, plus reinvested modern profits.

**Modern structural-change theory (Herrendorf, Rogerson & Valentinyi L024).** Labor moves agriculture → manufacturing → services because of income effects (non-homothetic demand) and relative-price effects (differential productivity growth with low substitutability). This produces the hump in manufacturing shares.

**Productivity gaps are real (Gollin, Lagakos & Waugh L026).** Value added per worker is much higher outside agriculture, especially in poor countries; the gap narrows but remains after adjusting for hours and human capital. So the reallocation channel is not a measurement artifact, though measured gaps overstate it.

**Services are where catch-up stalls (Duarte & Restuccia L025).** Cross-country productivity gaps are largest and most persistent in services. As economies grow, services' employment share rises, so the services gap increasingly governs aggregate productivity.

**Human capital drives reallocation (Porzio, Rossi & Santangelo L027).** About half of the global fall in agricultural employment is cohort replacement; human capital explains roughly 40% at fixed prices. Schooling expansion pushes people out of agriculture — but the theory is silent on *where they land*.

**Growth-reducing structural change in LAC (McMillan & Rodrik L018).** Since 1990, labor in LAC (and Africa) moved from higher- to lower-productivity activities. Resource-intensive exporters fared worse (resource sectors are high-productivity enclaves that absorb little labor); competitive real exchange rates and flexible labor markets helped.

**One channel at a time (Diao, McMillan & Rodrik L019).** No recent acceleration outside East Asia was industrialization-led. In LAC, growth came from within-sector productivity gains while reallocation contributed little or negatively; in Africa it was the reverse. The two channels rarely operate simultaneously — which is exactly what East Asian manufacturing achieved.

## What this strand gives the project

1. The shift-share decomposition: Δ(Y/L) = Σ within-sector productivity growth + Σ reallocation (static + dynamic).
2. The key LAC fact: *modernization and absorption are decoupled*. Modern segments raise productivity; displaced and new workers flow into low-productivity (mostly urban services, informal) activities.
3. A puzzle (see synthesis): firm-level evidence (IMF 2025, L041) says sector mix barely explains LAC's TFP shortfall, while sector-level evidence (L018) says reallocation has been growth-reducing. These are reconcilable only if the relevant "sectors" are not industries but *segments* (formal/informal, large/micro) that cut across industries. This motivates Idea 1 in `docs/theory/01_framework.md`.

## Limits

- Standard models have a representative firm per sector, so they cannot see within-sector dualism.
- Sector-level labor productivity mixes capital intensity with TFP; a high-productivity "enclave" (mining) can look like a good destination but cannot absorb labor.
- Demand-side constraints on absorption (who buys the output of upgraded services?) are under-theorized.
