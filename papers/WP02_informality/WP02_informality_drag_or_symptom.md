# Informality in Latin America: Drag on Growth or Symptom of Low Dynamism?

**devtheory Working Paper 02** · Draft v0.1 · 2 November 2026
Project: Productive Development in Latin America and the Caribbean ([repository README](../../README.md))

---

## Abstract

Almost half of Latin American workers are informal. Is that a cause of the region's slow growth or a consequence of it? This paper argues that the question as usually posed has no single answer, because "informality" bundles three different things. **Structural informality** — subsistence self-employment by people whose alternative is unemployment or migration — is mainly a *symptom*: it exists because productive firms do not create enough jobs, and policies that formalize it directly do little for growth. **Policy-induced informality** — firms and workers who would be formal under neutral rules but are pushed out by the way social insurance, taxes and labor law treat salaried formal employment — is a genuine *drag*: it misallocates labor and capital toward small, low-productivity units and keeps productive firms small. **Evasive informality** — firms productive enough to be formal that evade taxes and regulation — is also a *drag*, because it lets less productive firms survive by undercutting compliant ones. The three are linked by feedback loops: a large informal sector shrinks the tax base and raises the burden on formal jobs; few formal jobs mean little learning and few capable entrepreneurs; and both enlarge the informal sector again. Informality is therefore best understood as **a symptom that turns into a cause**. The evidence that formalization programs barely move informality — and that Chile, with informality near 25%, has stagnated too — shows that informality is not the root problem; the evidence on misallocation and tax wedges shows that it is not harmless either. The growth-relevant policy target is not the informality rate but the creation and expansion of productive formal firms, together with removing the specific policy wedges that tax them.

---

## 1. The question

Two views dominate public debate.

- **The drag view.** Informal firms are small, unproductive and untaxed. They pay no social contributions, compete unfairly with formal firms, absorb workers who would be more productive elsewhere and starve the state of revenue. Formalize them and productivity, revenue and growth will rise.
- **The symptom view.** Informality is what people do when the formal economy does not offer them a job. It shrinks as economies develop. Formalizing a street vendor does not make her more productive; growth makes informality disappear, not the other way round.

Both views have strong evidence behind them. The paper's task is to say *which parts of informality* each view describes, and how the parts interact.

---

## 2. Facts

**Size.** The ILO puts the regional informality rate at 47% of employed people in 2025, with large variation: around 25% in Chile and Uruguay, around 70% in Peru and Ecuador, and more than 80% in Bolivia. Informality fell only about 0.7 percentage points in the first half of 2025, which the ILO itself calls insufficient. In a typical developing economy the informal sector produces around 35% of GDP and employs around 70% of the labor force (Loayza 2016) — the gap between those two numbers *is* the productivity gap.

**Firms.** In Mexico, informal firms are over 90% of firms and around 55% of employment, and they are about 50% less productive than formal firms (Levy 2018). Using census data, Busso, Fazio & Levy (2012) find that legal, fully formal firms were only about 3% of firms but held about 30% of capital and produced about 31% of value added. Informality *and* illegality increased between 1998 and 2008, despite a decade of reforms.

**Mobility.** Few informal firms ever become formal or grow (La Porta & Shleifer 2014). Subsistence and transformational entrepreneurs are largely distinct populations, and only a negligible fraction move from one to the other (Schoar 2010).

**Voluntariness.** Perry et al. (2007) find that around two-thirds of independent informal workers in the region chose their status voluntarily and report being about as well off as in the formal jobs available to them, while most *informal salaried* workers would prefer a formal job — their informality reflects their employers' decisions.

**Correlates.** Across countries, pervasive informality goes with weaker investment and productivity, smaller fiscal resources and more poverty (World Bank 2021) — associations that both views can explain, which is why they settle nothing on their own.

**Cyclicality.** The informal sector acts as a buffer: it absorbs workers after negative labor-demand shocks and limits unemployment (Dix-Carneiro et al. 2021).

---

## 3. What exactly is "informality"? Four distinctions that matter

1. **Firm vs. worker informality.** An unregistered firm (extensive margin) is different from a registered firm that hires some workers off the books (intensive margin). Policies that target one often move the other.
2. **Legal vs. illegal informality.** In Mexico, a self-employed person or a firm using only non-salaried labor is informal *and legal*; a firm that hires salaried workers without paying social security is informal *and illegal* (Busso, Fazio & Levy 2012). Enforcement can only act on the second. Legal informality is a product of how the law is written.
3. **Subsistence vs. transformational activity.** Most informal units exist to provide a living, not to grow (Schoar 2010).
4. **Exit vs. exclusion.** Some workers and firms choose informality after weighing costs and benefits (exit); others are shut out of formal jobs (exclusion) (Perry et al. 2007).

From these, the paper groups informality into three types:

| Type | Who | Would they be formal under neutral rules and full enforcement? | Main explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structural** (S) | Subsistence self-employed, unpaid family workers, tiny units below any viable formal scale | No — they would be unemployed, inactive or migrants | Too few productive formal jobs (symptom) |
| **Policy-induced** (P) | Firms and workers just below the formality threshold because rules tax formal salaried work and subsidize alternatives | Yes | Social-insurance, tax and labor-law design (drag) |
| **Evasive** (E) | Firms productive enough to comply that evade taxes or contributions, fully or partly | Yes — they are formal-capable | Weak enforcement (drag) |

The debate between "drag" and "symptom" is largely a debate about the relative size of S versus P + E.

---

## 4. The symptom view: evidence

**(a) Cheap formalization barely formalizes.** Making registration simpler and cheaper raises the number of formal firms modestly at best; most microenterprises remain informal, and most informal firms do not appear to benefit on net from formalizing (Bruhn & McKenzie 2014). In Brazil, lower taxes for small firms did formalize some existing informal firms, but the formalization elasticity was low and the program produced net revenue losses (Rocha, Ulyssea & Rachter 2018). If most informality were P-type, cutting the cost of formality should have moved it a lot. It did not.

**(b) Informality falls with development everywhere.** It is highest in the poorest countries and lowest in the richest, across very different legal systems (Loayza 2016; La Porta & Shleifer 2014).

**(c) Firms of equal productivity sit on both sides of the line.** In Brazilian data, sector choice is not determined by productivity alone (Meghir, Narita & Robin 2015). The informal sector is not simply a "bad firms" sector.

**(d) It buffers shocks.** When labor demand falls, informality absorbs workers who would otherwise be unemployed (Dix-Carneiro et al. 2021). Eliminating that margin without creating formal jobs would raise unemployment, not productivity.

**(e) Chile.** Chile has one of the lowest informality rates in the region, around 25%. Yet the IMF's productivity decomposition for the five largest Latin American economies — Chile among them — shows negative average TFP growth in 2000–19, concentrated within surviving formal firms (IMF 2025; this project's synthesis, fact F2). If informality were the main cause of Latin America's stagnation, the least informal economy should have escaped it. It did not. Whatever is holding the region back also operates inside the formal sector.

---

## 5. The drag view: evidence and mechanisms

Informality can lower aggregate productivity through five channels.

**(1) Misallocation through the formality wedge.** When social contributions and labor rules apply to *salaried formal* employment only, they act as a tax on exactly the kind of jobs that productive firms create, and as a subsidy to non-salaried and informal arrangements — especially when non-contributory programs give informal workers similar benefits for free (Levy 2018). Resources then flow to the wrong firms. Busso, Fazio & Levy (2012) estimate that a peso of capital and labor in legal formal firms is worth 28% more than in illegal informal firms and 50% more than in legal informal firms. Model-based estimates of the TFP gain from removing all within-sector distortions are very large (roughly 200% in their estimates), but the robust message is the direction and the fact that the *policy-induced* part is substantial.

**(2) Unfair competition.** Evading firms (type E) undercut compliant firms on price and survive despite lower productivity. This keeps productive firms smaller than they would be and protects unproductive ones. A general-equilibrium model calibrated to Mexico finds that full tax enforcement would raise labor productivity and output by about 19% under perfect competition and 34% under monopolistic competition, by raising capital per worker in small firms, discouraging low-productivity entrepreneurs, and reallocating resources (Leal Ordóñez 2014). These are model counterfactuals, not observed effects, but they quantify how large the evasion channel could be.

**(3) Staying small to stay invisible.** Enforcement is stricter for larger firms, so firms have an incentive to stay below the radar. This flattens firm life cycles — one of the region's most robust facts (Hsieh & Klenow 2014; Eslava, Haltiwanger & Pinzón 2019) — and it means informality distorts the size distribution of *formal* firms as well.

**(4) A narrow tax base.** When half the workforce and a third of output are outside the tax net, the state collects less, provides fewer and worse public goods (infrastructure, courts, schooling quality), and taxes the formal minority more heavily. That raises the formality wedge in channel (1). This is the fiscal loop of the project's framework (Idea 4, loop 2).

**(5) No learning.** Informal jobs teach little: no structured training, no management hierarchy, little exposure to technology, little access to credit. Experience-wage profiles are about twice as steep in rich as in poor countries (Lagakos et al. 2018). A labor force that spends its working life in informal units accumulates less human capital and produces fewer experienced managers and entrepreneurs (framework Idea 3). This is a *dynamic* cost that static misallocation estimates miss.

**What enforcement does.** The structural literature finds that stronger enforcement on the *extensive* margin — against unregistered firms that could be formal — is the most effective formalization policy and can raise TFP and output (Ulyssea 2020); in a Brazilian labor-market model, tighter enforcement raises wages, output and welfare without raising unemployment (Meghir, Narita & Robin 2015). But the experimental evidence is more sobering. More than 480,000 random workplace inspections in Mexico show that raising the cost of informal workers formalizes some of them temporarily, but separations increase and inspected firms end up with *persistently lower formal employment* (Samaniego de la Parra & Fernández Bujanda 2024). Where firms' productivity is low, enforcement destroys jobs rather than formalizing them. That is exactly what one would expect if a large part of informality is S-type.

## 6. Synthesis: a symptom that becomes a cause

### 6.1 A simple segment-choice logic

Let a potential producer (self-employed person or firm) have productivity $z$. Producing formally requires paying a wedge $\tau$ (contributions, taxes, compliance costs net of the value of formal benefits) and gives access to scale, credit, contracts and public goods, which raise effective productivity by a factor $(1+\phi)$. Producing informally avoids $\tau$ but caps scale and faces an expected penalty $p$ that rises with visibility (size). A producer goes formal if

$$
(1+\phi)\,z\,(1-\tau) - c_f \;>\; z - p(z) ,
$$

which defines a productivity threshold $z^*(\tau, \phi, p, c_f)$. Informality is the mass of producers below $z^*$.

The three types map onto this immediately:

- **Structural (S):** producers with $z$ so low that they would stay informal even with $\tau = 0$ and strong enforcement. Their number is governed by the *distribution* of $z$ — that is, by how many productive firms and jobs exist. This is the symptom.
- **Policy-induced (P):** producers between the threshold under neutral rules and the actual $z^*$. Their number rises with $\tau$, i.e., with how heavily the system taxes formal salaried work relative to the alternatives.
- **Evasive (E):** producers above $z^*$ who evade because $p$ is low.

### 6.2 The loops

Three feedbacks turn the symptom into a cause:

1. **Fiscal loop:** large S + P + E → narrow tax base → higher $\tau$ on the formal minority and lower-quality public goods (lower $\phi$) → higher $z^*$ → more P.
2. **Capability loop:** few formal jobs → little on-the-job learning and few experienced managers → fewer capable entrepreneurs → a distribution of $z$ shifted left → more S.
3. **Competition loop:** many E-type firms → compliant productive firms grow less → fewer formal jobs → more S.

Hence the paper's answer: **most Latin American informality is, at any moment, the visible face of too few productive firms (a symptom); but a significant part is created by policy and tolerated evasion (a drag), and the loops mean that informality also reproduces the low dynamism that created it.** Mexico, where reforms that opened the economy were followed by *faster* growth of informal than formal firms (Levy 2018), is the clearest case of loops 1 and 3 at work. Chile, with low informality and stagnant TFP anyway, shows that eliminating informality would not by itself solve the growth problem.

### 6.3 How big is each type? (An empirical agenda)

The paper does not have a decomposition of informality into S, P and E, and the existing literature does not provide one. Four observable fingerprints can separate them in Phase 2:

| Type | Fingerprint |
|---|---|
| S | Low earnings close to subsistence; no employees; little response to formalization incentives; strongly countercyclical |
| P | Bunching just below size or revenue thresholds; responsiveness to changes in the formality wedge (contribution rates, non-contributory benefits) |
| E | Registered firms with under-reported employment or sales; responsiveness to audit and inspection probability; productivity comparable to formal firms |
| All | Informality in sectors and regions with fast formal-job growth falls mainly through new cohorts (people entering formal jobs) rather than incumbents formalizing |

---

## 7. What follows for policy (and what doesn't)

1. **Don't target the informality rate.** Formalizing subsistence units adds paperwork, not productivity. The growth-relevant target is the number of jobs in productive formal firms.
2. **Remove the wedge on formal salaried work.** The single most growth-relevant reform is to stop social insurance from taxing formal employment specifically — e.g., financing a universal core of social insurance from general revenue and broad-based consumption taxes, as Levy proposes for Mexico. This shrinks P-type informality and the misallocation it causes.
3. **Enforce where firms can comply.** Target enforcement at firms above the viability threshold (E-type) — the extensive margin of formal-capable firms — not at subsistence units, where inspections destroy jobs.
4. **Avoid size traps.** Simplified small-firm tax regimes with sharp thresholds create incentives to stay small; the IMF identifies size-based policies as one of three frictions behind Latin American misallocation. Phase-ins should be smooth.
5. **Grow the formal sector from the top.** Since informality shrinks mainly as productive firms expand and new cohorts enter formal jobs, the policies of WP01 — tradable, domestically embedded, labor-absorbing firms — matter more for informality than formalization programs do.

---

## 8. Propositions for the empirical phase

- **P13.** Informality falls in regions and sectors with fast growth of formal employment mainly through the entry of new cohorts into formal jobs, not through incumbents formalizing.
- **P14.** Increases in the wedge between contributory and non-contributory benefits raise informality mostly among small *salaried* employers (P-type), not among own-account workers (S-type). (Refines P8.)
- **P15.** Within narrowly defined sectors, firm-size distributions show bunching below enforcement and tax thresholds.
- **P16.** Holding sector and region constant, a higher informal employment share predicts slower productivity growth of *formal* firms in the same market (competition loop), after controlling for demand.
