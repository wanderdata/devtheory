# Foreign Firms, Rents and Growth: Who Keeps the Gains When a Multinational Invests?

**devtheory Working Paper 03** · Draft v0.1 · 2 November 2026
Project: Productive Development in Latin America and the Caribbean ([repository README](../../README.md))

---

## Abstract

When a multinational such as Coca-Cola, Walmart or a mining company invests in Latin America, it brings capital, technology, brands and management; it creates jobs, often better-paid ones; and it gives consumers products they want at lower prices. It also earns rents, part of which leave the country as dividends, royalties, fees and transfer prices, reducing the domestic income multiplier and drawing on scarce foreign exchange. This paper sets out the full ledger. It distinguishes five kinds of profit a foreign firm earns — a normal return on capital, a return on technology and brands, a resource rent, a market-power rent and shifted profit — and argues that only the last three are a development problem, each with its own remedy. Evidence from Latin America shows that the largest gains from foreign firms come through *suppliers* (Costa Rican firms that start selling to multinationals employ 26% more workers four years later) and *consumers* (foreign supermarket entry in Mexico produced large welfare gains, mostly through lower prices), while within-industry spillovers to domestic competitors are weak or negative. The outflow of rents matters most for foreign firms that sell only in the domestic market: they earn no foreign exchange but remit in foreign currency, tightening the external constraint that limits Latin American growth. Finally, the relevant comparison is not foreign versus domestic ownership but *rents reinvested at home versus rents taken abroad*: about half of FDI "inflows" to the region in 2025 were foreign firms reinvesting their own profits, while wealthy domestic owners in several Latin American countries hold very large fortunes offshore. A foreign firm is good for growth when it competes rather than monopolizes, links into local suppliers, pays tax where it produces, and either earns foreign exchange or reinvests locally.

## 1. The trade-off in one example

A global beverage brand enters a Latin American market. Consider what happens on each side.

**Gains:**
- Consumers get a product they value, reliably and at a price that reflects global scale.
- Bottling plants, trucks and distribution networks create formal jobs, typically paying more than the local average.
- Local suppliers (sugar, packaging, transport, retail) gain a large, stable customer with exacting standards.
- The state collects corporate and payroll taxes and consumption taxes on a formal, visible product.

**Leakages:**
- The brand owner is paid for its brand and formula. That payment leaves the country.
- Profits on foreign-owned capital are partly remitted abroad.
- Local competitors may be displaced, and with them domestic profits that would have stayed at home.

**The structure of the deal decides the balance.** In the Coca-Cola system in Latin America, much of the capital-intensive and labor-intensive work — bottling and distribution — is done by bottlers in which local capital holds large stakes. Coca-Cola FEMSA, for example, was 45.7% owned by Mexico's FEMSA and 39.6% by The Coca-Cola Company in 2003. The brand owner's rent comes mainly through the price of concentrate, which bottler agreements let The Coca-Cola Company set unilaterally as a percentage of the retail price (Coca-Cola FEMSA, SEC filing 2003; ownership shares have changed since and should be updated before citing). The rent leaves the country as a payment for an imported input, not as visible profit repatriation. Most jobs, most capital and a large share of the bottling profit stay in the country.

The example generalizes. **What matters is not whether a firm is foreign, but which parts of the value chain it controls, how it extracts its rent, and what happens to that rent.**

## 2. Five kinds of profit, five different problems

A foreign firm's profit in a host country is a mix of five components. They have different development implications and call for different responses.

| Component | What it pays for | Development problem? | Appropriate response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Normal return on capital** | Capital the country did not have or would have paid more for | No — it is the price of capital | None; ensure stable rules so the price stays low |
| **2. Return on technology, brand, know-how** | Knowledge the country could not produce or license cheaply | Generally no, if competitively priced | Encourage diffusion through suppliers and workers |
| **3. Resource rent** | Nothing — scarcity of a natural resource the nation owns | **Yes**, if left with the firm | Royalties and resource taxation |
| **4. Market-power rent** | Nothing — the absence of competition | **Yes** | Competition policy, open entry |
| **5. Shifted profit** | Nothing — tax avoidance via transfer prices, fees and intra-firm loans | **Yes** | Transfer-pricing rules, minimum taxes, information exchange |

The common complaint that "the profits leave" mixes all five. Components 1 and 2 are the price of what the firm brought. Leaving them with the firm is the condition for getting the investment at all. Components 3–5 are rents that the host country could keep without losing the investment.

**Profit shifting is large.** Close to 40% of multinational profits are shifted to tax havens globally. In low-tax countries, foreign affiliates appear about ten times more profitable than local firms (Tørsløv, Wier & Zucman 2023). The channels are exactly those through which a beverage, pharmaceutical or technology firm would extract a legitimate brand royalty: fees, royalties and the prices of intra-firm inputs. The line between component 2 and component 5 is therefore drawn by tax administrations, not by the firm's accounts.

## 3. The ledger, channel by channel

The ledger is the one from WP01: production, income and demand, and capability.

### 3.1 Jobs: how many, what kind, displacing whom?

- **Wage premium.** In Costa Rica, workers earn about 9% more at multinationals. The premium reflects above-market pay, not compensation for worse conditions. Exposure to multinationals also raises earnings growth for workers in *domestic* firms by about one percentage point per standard deviation of exposure (Alfaro-Ureña, Manelici & Vasquez 2021).
- **Net, not gross.** As WP01 argues, a job's contribution is its productivity minus the worker's alternative. In consumer markets, foreign entry often displaces domestic firms. In Mexico, foreign supermarket entry led traditional stores to exit, lowered domestic store profits and reduced the incomes of traditional retail workers. Average municipal income and employment barely changed (Atkin, Faber & Gonzalez-Navarro 2018). The gross jobs created by a foreign retailer overstate its net job creation.
- **Type of job.** Foreign firms are typically formal, larger and more capital-intensive. They create the M-segment jobs of the project's framework, with their learning content (Idea 3). But in non-tradable sectors they may create fewer jobs than they destroy.

### 3.2 Consumers: often the largest and least visible gain

Foreign supermarket entry in Mexico produced large, significant welfare gains for the average household, positive for every income group. The gains came mostly from lower living costs. About a quarter of the price effect came from domestic stores cutting prices under competition; the rest came from households shopping at the foreign stores (Atkin, Faber & Gonzalez-Navarro 2018). The gains appear specific to foreign entry rather than to modern formats in general.

This gain does not appear in GDP growth as such, but it raises real incomes. For consumer-facing multinationals it is often the main benefit, and it is lost if entry is blocked.

A caveat for products such as sugary drinks: "consumers get what they want" is weaker when the product carries health costs that consumers do not fully weigh. That is a case for corrective taxes on the product (as Mexico introduced on sugary drinks), whoever produces it. It is not a case against foreign ownership.

### 3.3 Suppliers: where the spillovers are

The strongest evidence of productivity spillovers runs through **backward linkages**: multinationals raise the productivity of their domestic *suppliers*.
- In Costa Rica, four years after their first sale to a multinational, domestic suppliers employ 26% more workers, have 4–9% higher TFP, and sell 20% more to *other* buyers, mostly new and better ones (Alfaro-Ureña, Manelici & Vasquez 2022).
- In Lithuania, effects are larger when the foreign investor shares ownership with local partners, because joint ventures source more locally (Javorcik 2004).
- **Horizontal spillovers** — to domestic competitors in the same industry — are weak or negative. In Venezuela, foreign joint ventures raised their own productivity but *lowered* that of domestic plants in the same industry, so the net effect was small (Aitken & Harrison 1999).
- In the aggregate, FDI's growth benefits depend on the host's absorptive capacity: FDI contributes more to growth than domestic investment only above a minimum level of human capital (Borensztein, De Gregorio & Lee 1998).

**Implication:** the developmental value of a foreign firm depends far more on *how much it buys locally* than on how much it invests.

### 3.4 Rents and the domestic multiplier

The "growth multiplier" concern is correct in mechanism. Profits remitted abroad generate no domestic consumption or investment. Profits kept by domestic owners generate some, *if* those owners spend or invest at home.

Table 1 makes the comparison explicit. It shows five stylized firms that each generate US$100 of value added (wages 35, taxes 15, profits 50 before fees), produced by [`rent_leakage.py`](rent_leakage.py). The parameters are illustrative orders of magnitude, listed in the script.

**Table 1. Where US$100 of value added goes, by ownership and market (illustrative)**

| Case | Taxes | Fees/royalties abroad | Profits repatriated | Reinvested at home | Domestic capital flight | National income (GNI view) | 1st-round domestic demand | Net foreign exchange |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Foreign brand, sells at home (beverage-type) | 15.0 | 15 | 21.0 | 14.0 | 0 | 50.0 | 32.0 | −41.6 |
| Foreign firm, exports (assembly/mining-type) | 15.0 | 5 | 27.0 | 18.0 | 0 | 50.0 | 34.4 | +60.8 |
| Foreign firm, profit shifting via transfer prices | 11.2 | 30 | 19.0 | 4.8 | 0 | 46.2 | 26.5 | −50.9 |
| Domestic firm, owners reinvest at home | 15.0 | 0 | 0 | 30.0 | 0 | 100.0 | 47.1 | −12.0 |
| Domestic firm, owners move savings offshore | 15.0 | 0 | 0 | 10.0 | 20.0 | 100.0 | 35.1 | −24.0 |

What the table shows:

1. **Wages and taxes are the anchor.** In every case the country keeps the 35 in wages and (most of) the 15 in taxes. That is why national income from a foreign firm is about half its value added here. It is also why the tax rate on foreign profits — and the defense of the tax base against shifting — decides how much of a foreign firm's value added the country keeps.
2. **Profit shifting is the worst case on every column.** It costs taxes, national income, domestic demand and foreign exchange at the same time.
3. **A domestic owner who moves savings offshore leaks almost as much domestic demand as a foreign owner** (35.1 vs. 32.0 first-round demand). National income is higher, because offshore assets still belong to residents, but the investment that would raise future output does not happen at home. This is not a hypothetical concern for the region. Offshore wealth is around 10% of world GDP, but it approaches 60% of GDP in some Latin American and Gulf countries (Alstadsæter, Johannesen & Zucman 2018).
4. **Foreign firms reinvest a lot.** In 2025, reinvested earnings were about US$93 billion, roughly half of the region's US$194 billion in FDI inflows (ECLAC 2026). Much of what is reported as "new foreign investment" is foreign firms reinvesting profits earned in the region. Those profits are still claims of foreigners, but the capital stays.

### 3.5 Foreign exchange: the decisive difference between exporters and domestic sellers

The most important column in Table 1 is the last. A foreign firm that **exports** earns foreign exchange. Its remittances are paid out of dollars it brings in, so it relaxes the external constraint even after remitting (+60.8 in the table). A foreign firm that **sells only at home** — beverages, retail, telecoms, banking, utilities — earns pesos but remits dollars. Its royalties, dividends and imported inputs must be paid with foreign exchange earned by *someone else's* exports (−41.6).

In an economy whose growth is limited by the balance of payments (WP01, §3.3, Thirlwall's law), domestic-market FDI therefore makes a growing claim on a scarce resource. FDI arrives once as an inflow. If the investment is profitable, it then generates an income outflow every year. Over time, the stock of foreign-owned capital in domestic-market sectors converts into a permanent current-account obligation. It is sustainable only if the country's tradable sectors grow fast enough to pay it.

This is the precise sense in which foreign ownership can lower the growth "multiplier" of the economy. The problem is not that profits leave once; it is that a rising share of national output becomes claims payable in foreign currency, while the activity generating them earns none.

## 4. Which foreign firms are good for growth?

Combining the channels, a foreign investment contributes most to growth when it:

1. **Brings something the country lacks** — capital at lower cost, technology, market access, management — so that its profit is mostly components 1 and 2 of Section 2.
2. **Competes rather than monopolizes.** Consumer gains come from competition (a quarter of Mexico's supermarket price gains came through domestic competitors cutting prices). Entry into concentrated markets is good; acquiring a dominant domestic incumbent and its market power is not.
3. **Buys locally.** Backward linkages are where productivity spillovers happen. Joint ventures and supplier-development programs raise local sourcing.
4. **Pays tax where it produces**, so that its national-income contribution is not eroded by shifting.
5. **Earns foreign exchange or reinvests locally.** Export-oriented FDI pays its own way; domestic-market FDI should reinvest or be matched by export growth elsewhere.
6. **Creates net M-segment jobs**, not only gross jobs.

Ranked by type, using these criteria:
- **Export-oriented FDI with local linkages scores highest.** Examples: electronics and medical devices in Costa Rica with domestic suppliers, agribusiness, and mining with a domestic supplier base.
- **Consumer-market FDI in competitive markets comes next.** Examples: retail and beverages with high local sourcing and full tax compliance. Its gains are mostly consumer gains and supplier upgrading, and it costs foreign exchange.
- **FDI that acquires market power, captures resource rents without adequate royalties, or shifts profits scores lowest.**

## 5. Why not keep foreign firms out? The counterfactual

The alternative to foreign ownership is not a domestic firm with identical technology, brand and capital. The realistic alternatives are:
- a less productive domestic firm, with higher prices for consumers;
- a domestic monopoly protected by entry barriers, with domestic rents and no competitive discipline;
- no production at all, and imports.

Latin America's import-substitution period tried versions of all three and produced high-cost, inward-looking producers. Moreover, domestic ownership does not guarantee domestic reinvestment, as the region's offshore wealth shows. The policy question is therefore not *whether* to allow foreign firms but **how to shift the composition of their profit toward components 1 and 2, and their spending toward local suppliers and reinvestment**. That means:
- competition policy that applies to foreign and domestic incumbents alike;
- resource royalties;
- transfer-pricing enforcement and participation in global minimum taxation;
- supplier-development programs (Costa Rica and Chile's mining supplier initiatives are the regional models);
- a macro framework that keeps the export sector growing fast enough to service the foreign-owned capital stock.

## 6. Propositions for the empirical phase

- **P17.** Domestic firms that become suppliers to multinationals in LAC show persistent gains in employment, productivity and sales to other buyers (replicating Costa Rica in Mexico, Brazil, Chile and Colombia), and the gains are larger when the multinational has local partners.
- **P18.** Within-industry spillovers from foreign entry to domestic competitors are zero or negative on productivity but positive on consumer prices.
- **P19.** FDI income outflows from domestic-market sectors (retail, telecoms, finance, utilities, consumer goods) are a growing share of LAC current-account debits, and countries where these sectors' share of the foreign-owned capital stock is higher face tighter external constraints on growth.
- **P20.** Effective tax rates on foreign affiliates in LAC fall with the share of intra-firm fees and royalties in their costs (profit-shifting channel).
