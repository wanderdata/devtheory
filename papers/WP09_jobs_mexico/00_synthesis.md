# Jobs and Productive Development in Mexico: Findings of Six Studies, 2005–2026

*Paul Wander. Working draft v0.1, 2026-11-16. Synthesis of WP09 studies 9.1–9.6; each finding cites the study in which it is documented. The studies were checked by an independent audit whose corrections are recorded in decision D-064.*

## Abstract

Earlier work in this project found no sector whose share of output reliably predicts growth (WP07) and found that sector averages conceal most of the variation in productivity (WP06, WP08). Programme WP09 took the job as the unit of analysis and used Mexico's labour-force survey (ENOE), with its five-quarter rotating panel, to ask which jobs are associated with stable employment, earnings growth with experience and transitions to formal or better-paid work. Across six studies, the characteristics of jobs that labour-force surveys record (sector, establishment size and formality) are weak guides to these outcomes. Together they explain 3–7% of the variance of individual transitions within a year (9.1); experience–earnings profiles are flat in formal and informal jobs alike, and formal profiles have flattened since 2005 (9.2); and the 2019 doubling of the minimum wage in the northern border zone moved earnings at the bottom of the distribution without detectably changing transitions out of informality (9.4). The clearest associations are with the formality of a young person's first job (9.5) and with the state in which a person works: state differences in mobility, at given workforce composition, are large and stable, but only weakly related to subsequent growth (9.6). Splitting sectors into formal and informal segments does not change WP08's verdicts on productive development, and the contribution it attributes to formalisation depends on an assumed productivity gap (9.3).

## 1. Question and approach

The motivation for WP09 was a negative result. If no sector reliably acts as a motor of growth, the relevant variation may lie below the sector, in the jobs that people hold. A job could matter for development in three ways: by keeping its holder employed, by raising earnings with experience, and by leading to better jobs. The programme asked which observable characteristics of jobs are associated with each of these, and whether places and policies change them.

All six studies use ENOE microdata, which INEGI publishes quarterly from 2005, and the same linkage of persons across interviews (match rates of 88–93% between consecutive quarters). Each study set out its propositions and decision rules in a pre-analysis plan before its estimates were computed. The results are descriptive except in study 9.4, which uses a difference-in-differences design whose identifying assumption is stated there. An independent audit of all six studies led to corrections and additional analyses (D-064), which are reflected below.

## 2. Characteristics of jobs

Study 9.1 follows persons aged 16–65 employed at a first interview for one year, in 2018–19 and 2023–24, and compares how much of the variation in two outcomes, exit to non-employment and a move to a better-paid job cell, is explained by sector and by establishment size × formality. Size × formality explains more than sector of both outcomes in the simplest specification, but the advantage for exits disappears when transitions must be confirmed at an adjacent interview, and the advantage for upward moves depends on establishment size, which a third of employees with the same employer report differently a year later. When upward moves are defined on changes of employer, the advantage is 0.5 points of R². All groupings together explain about 3% of the variance of exits and 7% of upward moves. Manufacturing jobs show more upward moves than service jobs in the same type of establishment, but this reflects their lower starting position in the earnings ranking.

Study 9.2 estimates experience–earnings profiles for full-time male private-sector wage employees, 2005–2025. Hourly earnings at 20–24 years of potential experience are 25.7% above those at 0–4 years, against about 89% in rich countries in Lagakos et al. (2018); 25.4% in formal and 15.6% in informal jobs. In manufacturing and commerce, the two sectors with enough informal wage employees for separate estimates, formal and informal profiles are similar, and differences across sectors exceed the pooled formality gap. The formal profile fell from 35.6% in 2005–2009 to 18.6% in 2020–2025. Reweighting for earnings non-response raises the heights by 0.6–5.6 points without changing these comparisons.

Taken together, the two studies do not identify a type of job, as recorded in the survey, that combines stability, earnings growth with experience and upward mobility. Formality is associated with lower exit rates (5.5–10.6% a year in formal size classes against 10.9–23.1% in informal ones), but not, within the sectors where it can be tested, with steeper earnings profiles.

## 3. Entry into employment

Study 9.5 identifies first jobs from respondents' statements that they had never worked, across 70 five-quarter panels from 2005–2019 and 2022–2026 (47,186 entrants aged 15–24). Of first jobs, 85.1% are informal, against 68.5% of all jobs of employed 15–24-year-olds. Three quarters after entry, about half of informal entrants and 36% of formal entrants are not employed. Conditional on observed characteristics, entering an informal job is associated with a probability of formal employment three quarters later that is 3.4 points higher than remaining without work, on a base of 2.5%, and entering a formal rather than an informal job with a 34.0-point higher probability. The first association is robust to substantial selection on unobservables; the second is not.

Of the differences between types of job examined in the programme, the one between formal and informal first jobs is the largest. Whether it reflects the jobs or the people who obtain them cannot be settled with the survey.

## 4. Places

Study 9.6 estimates one-year mobility rates for each of the 32 states, adjusted for age, sex, schooling, sector and urban residence, from 2.8 million persons observed a year apart. In panels starting in 2005–2011 the adjusted rate at which informally employed persons become formally employed ranges from 6.3% in Querétaro to 19.1% in Guerrero, around a mean of 12.3%, and sampling error accounts for about 2% of the variance across states. State rates are highly correlated across periods (0.96 between 2005–2011 and 2012–2018) and are not related to the size of the formal sector. Across 30 states, mobility in 2005–2011 has a rank correlation of 0.32 (90% interval 0.01–0.55) with growth of GDP per person in 2012–2019, but none with growth over 2005–2019 as a whole.

At given workforce composition, mobility therefore differs persistently between places, but its relation to growth is weak and depends on the period over which growth is measured.

## 5. Policy and productivity accounting

Study 9.4 examines the 2019 doubling of the minimum wage in the northern border zone, which came with tax cuts. Comparing zone municipalities with the rest of the six border states, the employment rate did not fall (0.9 points, interval −0.1 to 1.8) and the quarterly rate of transition from informal to formal employment did not rise (−1.2 points, interval −5.0 to 1.6, on a base of 18.5%). Counting answers given in ranges of the minimum wage, the share of wage employees below the new minimum fell by 11.8 points. A large increase in the wage floor raised pay at the bottom of the earnings distribution without detectably changing who holds formal jobs within a year.

Study 9.3 applies WP08's productivity ladder to Mexico with formal and informal segments within sectors. The dominance verdicts are the same as with one level per sector, so the finer split does not resolve WP08's ambiguous comparisons. The decomposition attributes to the reallocation of workers from informal to formal segments about a quarter of the sector-level within-sector term over 2005–2019, but that contribution is proportional to the assumed productivity gap and needs calibration with INEGI's informal-economy accounts before it can be read as a measurement.

## 7. Measurement

Several features of ENOE affect any analysis of jobs with these data. Establishment size is reported inconsistently: a third of employees with an unchanged employer report a different size class a year later (9.1). Earnings reporting has fallen steadily, from about 90% of formal wage employees in 2005 to 57% in 2025 (9.2), and fell further in the border zone after the 2019 minimum-wage increase, mostly through refusals (9.4). INEGI enlarged the sample in the border zone in 2019Q1, at the date of the policy (9.4). Some files carry encoding marks and changed variable names, which the shared reader now handles. The pre-2020 survey weights follow population projections based on the 2010 census (OQ-79).

## 8. Implications and further work

For the analysis of productive development, the programme reinforces the conclusion of WP07 from a different angle. Sector does not identify motors of growth, and the job characteristics that household surveys record do not identify motor jobs either. The variation that matters for workers' trajectories appears to lie in characteristics these surveys measure poorly or not at all, such as the employer, the occupation within the establishment and the worker's skills, and in the places where people work. Two kinds of data would test this directly: linked employer–employee records, such as IMSS records for formal employment, which identify employers and follow workers for many years; and comparable rotating panels in other countries of the region (Brazil's PNAD Contínua, Argentina's EPH, Chile's ENE), which would show whether Mexico's patterns are general. Within Mexico, the most immediate extension is the calibration of the formal–informal productivity gap with INEGI's informal-economy accounts (OQ-68), whose method is documented in the project's source files.
